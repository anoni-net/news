## 2026 {#y2026}

A post you publish on X can be hidden only from users in a particular country, in response to a court order or other valid legal demand from that country or because the post violates local law, and X's system assigns you a country based on location information it may receive (according to X's help page as saved by the Internet Archive on 20 December 2025, checked on 15 November 2026). In India, Section 69A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, in the version reproduced in the UNODC legislation database, lets the central government order intermediaries in writing to block public access to information on grounds such as national security and public order; the section itself does not mention a court order (procedures follow separate rules), and an intermediary that does not comply can be imprisoned for up to seven years (we checked the text on 15 November 2026). On 20 November 2000, the Paris court of first instance (Tribunal de grande instance) issued an urgent interim order giving Yahoo Inc in the United States three months from notification to comply with the court's ruling of 22 May that year by making Nazi memorabilia auctions on yahoo.com inaccessible to users in France, with a fine of 100,000 francs for each day of delay after that (according to the text of the order in the legal database Legalis). The three experts appointed by the court estimated in their report that, looking at the IP address alone (the number an internet provider assigns to each connection), about 70% of the addresses allocated to French users could be correctly identified as coming from France (OONI, the internet censorship measurement project covered in [our story the same day](https://anoni.net/news/en/2026/11/ooni-tls-middlebox/), also has an experiment that maps routers' IP addresses to countries), and two of them estimated that also asking users with unclear addresses to declare their nationality could probably bring the share blocked close to 90%, while the third considered filtering by location not very feasible because users can lie about where they are and because of privacy concerns. Yahoo banned the auction of Nazi items worldwide from January 2001, and company staff said this had nothing to do with the French ruling (the plaintiff groups said their lawsuit had brought it about, and The Christian Science Monitor wrote that legal experts generally believed the ruling had), while on 7 November 2001 the US District Court for the Northern District of California held that the First Amendment to the US Constitution, which protects freedom of speech, did not allow the ruling to be enforced in the United States.
