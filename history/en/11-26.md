## 2026 {#y2026}

In its Decree on Peace of November 1917, Russia's new government announced that it was abolishing secret diplomacy. When its foreign affairs commissioner published the treaties that the Tsarist government and the Provisional Government had each concluded with the Allies, his statement said, "We have nothing to hide." On 26 November, under the headline "RUSSIA AND SECRET TREATIES. / TERMS PUBLISHED.", the Manchester Guardian carried a Reuters dispatch from Petrograd relaying a summary from the new government's telegraph agency, which listed Russia's claims to Constantinople and France's to Alsace and Lorraine. According to the book The First Casualty, The Times printed the summary but decided "not to inconvenience the British, French and Italian Governments" and, "as far as possible, to curtail its Petrograd correspondent's despatches on the subject". When a member of the House of Commons asked for a statement on 29 November, the government answered, "I am not prepared to make any such statement at present," and when asked whether the documents were authentic, replied, "Some of them may be authentic and some not."

The treaties of 1917 were published by an incoming government from its predecessors' diplomatic archives, whereas Taiwan's current Classified National Security Information Protection Act (國家機密保護法) and Archives Act (檔案法) require classified national security information and national archives to be made available for access within 30 years at the latest. As of 22 November 2026, both laws allow that period to be extended in special circumstances with the consent of the Legislative Yuan (Taiwan's parliament), and classified information involving national security intelligence sources or channels is not bound by the 30-year limit when an extension of its classification is requested. In mainland China, the Law on Guarding State Secrets (保守国家秘密法), in its revised version in force since 1 May 2024, limits classification to 30, 20 or 10 years depending on the level, unless otherwise provided. A secret is declassified automatically when its period expires, and an extension must be decided before then by the classifying body or its superior. In Taiwan, people can apply under the Archives Act to read, transcribe or copy an agency's records, and the agency may not refuse without a legal basis, though it may refuse when the records involve national secrets.
