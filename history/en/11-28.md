## 2026 {#y2026}

The New York Times reported in 2010 that most US diplomatic cables remained secret for decades, and the State Department's unclassified history series had at that time reached only 1976. On 28 November 2010, WikiLeaks began publishing 251,287 cables, and some of the first 220 to go online had been redacted by its five partner newspapers to remove names that could identify diplomats' sources. A White House statement called the release a "reckless and dangerous action" and said that publishing the cables in full could put confidential sources at risk. WikiLeaks wrote on its page that citizens in a democracy should ask to see what is going on behind the scenes. According to a case study by the Columbia University Graduate School of Journalism, The Guardian sometimes cut place names and dates as well as names when redacting, and deleted pronouns where they could reveal that a speaker was a woman.

NPR, citing Der Spiegel, reported that an encrypted file of the full set of cables had been online since early 2011, and after WikiLeaks published the complete cables without redactions that September, the five newspapers condemned the decision in a joint statement. NPR also reported, citing The Wall Street Journal, that WikiLeaks claimed The Guardian had published the password to the encrypted file in a book, while according to The Guardian's own report, the paper had understood the file to be temporary and online for only a few hours. As of 23 November 2026, the cables can still be searched on the WikiLeaks website, and in tests since August by OONI (Open Observatory of Network Interference), a censorship measurement project, 1,121 of 1,196 tests in Taiwan reached the site normally. In mainland China, 14 of 301 did and 193 were flagged as anomalies, which does not by itself confirm blocking. Before sharing a screenshot of a conversation or a document, you can follow the practice the newspapers used for the cables and black out the place names and dates that could identify someone, not only their names.
