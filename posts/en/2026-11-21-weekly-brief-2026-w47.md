There was no issue in weeks 45 and 46, so this one collects eight stories from both weeks, two of them follow-ups to earlier articles. The reports and announcements are dated between 2 and 14 November.

## Two-factor authentication after Humble Bundle's redesign {#humble-bundle-2fa}

According to a PC Gamer report on 12 November, the newly launched website of Humble Bundle, a digital game store, has no two-factor authentication (2FA, a one-time code entered at login in addition to the password). Users cannot add it, and accounts that had it set up can log in with just an email address and password. A Privacy Guides article on 13 November added that changing the password no longer requires verification, and changing the email address no longer sends a confirmation to the original inbox. The PC Gamer writer recommended removing any payment details saved in the account, and noted that some users reported difficulty doing so.

According to a Notebookcheck report on 14 November, some users say two-factor authentication is working again. Notebookcheck also wrote that there has been no official confirmation that it has been restored for everyone. As of 15 November, we had found no official statement from Humble Bundle.

## Mistaken DMARC rejections at Outlook.com {#outlook-dmarc-rejections}

According to a blog post by the email provider MailRoute, from 5 November Microsoft's consumer mail services, including Outlook.com, Hotmail and Live, rejected some mail from domains without a DMARC record. DMARC is a sender authentication policy that a domain publishes in DNS, and together with SPF and DKIM (two other sender authentication mechanisms) it lets the receiving side confirm that a message really comes from that domain. Most of the affected domains already had SPF set up, and many also had DKIM. On 6 November MailRoute initially read the rejections as Microsoft requiring every sender to set up DMARC, and withdrew that reading in an update on 10 November.

The Register cited Microsoft's incident report `EX1545193`, which attributes the problem to a spam rule update that wrongly flagged and blocked mail. According to Microsoft's account as relayed by MailRoute, the update has been rolled back and the rejections ended at 11:02 UTC (19:02 Taipei time) on 9 November. Expert Insights quoted a Microsoft spokesperson as saying senders do not need to change their settings.

Under Microsoft's 2025 announcement, only domains sending more than 5,000 messages a day to Outlook.com have had to pass SPF, DKIM and DMARC since 5 May 2025. MailRoute warned that Outlook.com does not retry rejected mail, so important messages bounced between 5 and 9 November need to be sent again.

## Tails 7.17 and Tor Browser 16.0.1 and 16.0.2 {#tails-tor-browser}

Our 5 November article, "Tor Browser 16.0 and Tails 7.16", covered the previous versions of both. According to the Tor Project's announcement on 10 November, Tor Browser 16.0.1 includes important security updates to Firefox, rebasing onto Firefox 153.7.0esr and bringing in security fixes from Firefox 160. Tor itself is updated to 0.4.9.16.

The same day brought an Android-only 16.0.2, which fixes a breakage on Android caused by the zlib (compression library) update in 16.0.1. Neither announcement lists CVE numbers for individual vulnerabilities.

According to Tails' announcement on 11 November, version 7.17 updates Tor Browser to 16.0.1, Tor to 0.4.9.16 and the Linux kernel to 6.12.116. Tails 7.0 or later can upgrade to 7.17 automatically. Reinstalling instead erases the Persistent Storage on the USB stick.

## Mullvad Browser 16.0 {#mullvad-browser-16}

According to release notes published on 6 November by Mullvad, a VPN provider, Mullvad Browser 16.0 is the first version based on Firefox ESR 153, with a settings page redesigned along the lines of Firefox's June overhaul. There is now also a native build for Linux computers with 64-bit ARM processors, such as the Raspberry Pi. A new generic window title option replaces every window title with "Mullvad Browser" when turned on, because other programs on the computer and the operating system can read window titles without special permissions.

The release notes say the option is off by default because there was not enough time in this release cycle to evaluate side effects, and turning it on may make the browser harder to use for people who rely on accessibility tools such as screen readers. Our 5 November article covered the same option in Tor Browser 16.0.

The encrypted DNS settings are reduced to two choices, using DoH (DNS over HTTPS, which encrypts DNS queries before sending them) or using the operating system's DNS, which Mullvad recommends with a VPN. Our 30 September article, "An Android VPN leak and the end of Mullvad's public encrypted DNS", covered Mullvad shutting down its public encrypted DNS service. According to these release notes, browsers that used that service will be moved automatically to Quad9, a non-profit public DNS service. Customized DoH settings will not be changed, and anyone who manually chose a variant of Mullvad's encrypted DNS (base or family, for example) should switch back to the default option.

## The ChatGPT Lockdown Mode bypass {#chatgpt-lockdown-mode}

According to an article on 5 November by Check Point Research, the research arm of the security company Check Point, Lockdown Mode is an optional advanced security setting for logged-in ChatGPT users. Turning it on restricts live web access, stops images from the internet from loading and disables file downloads, among other things, cutting down the ways data can leave a conversation. The researchers found that the HTML preview OpenAI added to code blocks in February could open pop-up windows free of the sandbox (the mechanism that isolates web content and limits what it can do), bypassing Lockdown Mode's protections.

In ChatGPT Classic, the desktop app built on the Electron framework, the attack needs no action from the user. The researchers demonstrated a malicious custom GPT (a version of ChatGPT that users can build and share) quietly sending messages from the conversation to an attacker's server. In the browser, the phishing and malware delivery scenarios require the user to click a preview the attacker has designed.

According to Check Point's account, OpenAI told the researchers on 18 September that it had removed the pop-up permissions from the preview sandbox. The researchers retested and confirmed that the technique no longer works. The article does not ask users to update any software. As of 15 November, we had found no public statement from OpenAI about the vulnerability.

## Zcash's NU7 upgrade {#zcash-nu7}

According to a ForkLog report on 6 November, the mainnet of Zcash, a privacy-focused cryptocurrency, activated the NU7 network upgrade that day at block height 3,543,405, cutting the target block interval from 75 seconds to 25. As the report's example puts it, an exchange that requires three confirmations would credit deposits in about 75 seconds instead of 225. Miners' share of transaction fees drops to 40%, and the other 60% is taken out of circulation for now, to be paid out gradually as extra block rewards from around February 2031.

ForkLog also wrote that NU7 disables v4 transactions, the only format that can withdraw from Sprout, the first-generation shielded transaction system. About 12,224 ZEC left in Sprout can therefore no longer be spent, and NU7 comes with no recovery mechanism. The release notes for Zebra 7.0.0, the node software the Zcash Foundation published on 22 October, warn that mainnet nodes must upgrade before the activation height to stay on the consensus chain. ZIP 259, the NU7 deployment specification, also states that nodes that have not upgraded will remain on the old chain.

## Books from Taiwan seized by Hong Kong customs, and a national security arrest {#hk-seized-books}

According to a Hong Kong Free Press report on 4 November, a group that supports people in prison announced on social media on 2 November that two boxes of books it had ordered from Taiwan were down to half a box when they reached its partner retail outlet on 20 October. More than 60 books were missing.

According to the same report, the customs notice to the logistics firm cited new rules added in March to the Beijing-imposed national security law for Hong Kong, which allow customs officers to seize items suspected of having seditious intention even when no one has been arrested. The group's statement said the seized books were rational discussions of current affairs and personal experiences, with no connection to seditious intention. Hong Kong Free Press described this as the first known case of books being confiscated without any accompanying arrest.

According to a police statement relayed by The Standard, the National Security Department of the Hong Kong Police Force arrested a man in Mong Kok on the morning of 5 November on suspicion of importing publications with seditious intention under the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance, which Hong Kong enacted locally. The offence carries a maximum sentence of seven years in prison. Hong Kong Free Press reported on 6 November that the arrested man is understood to be a project manager at the same group.

According to the South China Morning Post, a customs spokesperson said the department would not comment on individual cases, and would detain items suspected of having seditious intention in accordance with the law and refer cases to the National Security Department where appropriate. According to the same report, the man was still being held for questioning as of the evening of 5 November. As of 15 November, we had found no reports of charges or bail.

## A German ministerial draft narrowing the Freedom of Information Act {#germany-ifg}

Germany's federal Freedom of Information Act (IFG) lets anyone request access to official information held by federal authorities. According to a netzpolitik.org report on 5 November, the freedom of information platform FragDenStaat published a ministerial draft (Referentenentwurf, a version drawn up by a ministry and not yet sent to the cabinet) of the Federal Ministry of the Interior's Transparency Adjustment Act (Transparenz-Anpassungsgesetz). The draft would exclude a number of policy areas classed as "critical infrastructure" from the scope of the Act. In a report on 10 November, netzpolitik.org cited FragDenStaat as listing the excluded areas as energy, transport, finance, health, water, food, information technology and telecommunications.

According to an article by Human Rights Watch (HRW) on 11 November, the government put forward the draft on 30 September, and it leaked on 5 November. An earlier attempt at narrowing the Act in June failed in the face of opposition from civil society and from the Social Democrats (SPD), a member of the governing coalition.

netzpolitik.org's 10 November report also gathered the critics' views. The federal managing director of the journalists' union dju said that shutting journalists out from the start is a direct attack on press freedom. A head of division at the office of the Federal Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information wrote in a statement that the draft's exclusions go too far and are legally questionable.

HRW argued in its article that the current law already rules out disclosing information that could endanger public security, making the draft's restrictions unnecessary. As of 15 November, we had found no response from the Federal Ministry of the Interior to these criticisms, and no information on which stage of the process the draft has reached.
