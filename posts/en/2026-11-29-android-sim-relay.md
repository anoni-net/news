For people who take calls on Taiwanese numbers, a domestic number on caller ID does not mean the caller is in Taiwan. In a set of cases announced on 19 November, Taiwan's Criminal Investigation Bureau (CIB) described scam rings that combined ordinary Android phones with an app and a few accessories into equipment that relays calls in both directions. Once a SIM card from a Taiwanese carrier is inserted, a scam call centre abroad can connect to the equipment remotely and place calls through it, posing as government agencies, hospitals or telecom customer service. Between July and September, police seized 18 sets of relay equipment, including phone-based ones.

When Taiwan's telecom carriers rolled out international call voice alerts across the board, playing a recorded warning before a call from abroad connects, overseas scam rings began setting up DMT units (digital mobile trunks) inside Taiwan to get around them. A DMT takes an overseas call that arrives over the internet and places it again as a mobile call from a Taiwanese SIM card inside the unit. According to iThome, a call placed this way looks like an ordinary domestic call, without the "+" and country code that a call from abroad usually begins with. After police seized purpose-built DMT equipment in large numbers, the rings switched to Android phones.

With offers of work from home, minding equipment or loans, or by posing as police and prosecutors who need help with an investigation, scam rings get members of the public to take in the equipment and insert SIM cards registered in their own names, so the number on caller ID may be the recruit's own. Anyone asked to put their SIM card into a device someone else supplied, or to keep such a device running at home, is being recruited in this way. People who agree may be billed for large call charges, and may also face criminal liability and civil claims. According to the CIB's warning, government agencies, police and prosecutors never ask the public to sign up for a phone number, hand over a SIM card, install an unknown app or set up communications equipment on their behalf.

## Perspective {#perspective}

From a caller ID number, all you can learn is which SIM card in Taiwan placed the call. The CIB classes the phone-based setups as DMT relay equipment as well, and iThome's report describes calls placed through a DMT as showing up as domestic calls. Putting the two together, a call relayed through an Android phone would also show the domestic number of the SIM card inside it. Who is calling, and from where, cannot be worked out from the number.

When someone claiming to be from a government agency, a hospital or telecom customer service calls, you can hang up first and then dial a number you have found yourself, from the official website, a bill or the back of a card. Do not call back the number on caller ID, or a number the caller gives you. Since the equipment relays calls in both directions, a call back to that domestic number could be routed to the call centre abroad. If in doubt, people in Taiwan can also call the 165 anti-fraud hotline.

In Hong Kong, calls from outside the territory carry a "+" prefix on caller ID, even when the number is disguised as a local one, a rule the Office of the Communications Authority (OFCA) has applied since August 2015. Since May 2023, all mobile service providers have also added a free voice or text alert to incoming calls showing "+852". When the alerts were announced, an OFCA spokesperson added that a call showing an 8-digit Hong Kong number with no "+" may still be a scam.

In mainland China, people can switch off incoming calls and texts from abroad, and as of June 2026 that service had been used 587 million times, according to figures from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) reported by the Beijing News. By the end of 2025, the telecom sector had dealt with nearly 200 million high-risk phone cards linked to fraud and helped police seize 210,000 "SIM-card cluster devices" used to place scam calls, commonly called cat pools.

As of 29 November, neither Scam Detection nor Call Screen, which screens a call before you answer, is offered in Taiwan in Google's Phone app, and Scam Detection is limited to Pixel phones. Android users who get these calls in Taiwan still have to rely on hanging up and checking the number independently.

On an iPhone, under Settings > Apps > Phone, calls from unknown numbers can be set to Ask Reason for Calling or Silence. With the first, the iPhone asks the caller to say why they are calling before it rings; with the second, the call is silenced and sent to voicemail. Both options work from whether the number is saved in your contacts, so they only cut down interruptions from unknown callers and cannot pick out a scam call placed from a domestic number. People who rarely need to answer unknown numbers might consider either option, at the cost of possibly missing legitimate calls from hospitals, couriers and other unknown numbers.
