South Korea's National Assembly Science, ICT, Broadcasting and Communications Committee (과학기술정보방송통신위원회, the science committee) is reviewing two biodata bills. At the same time, the Health and Welfare Committee (보건복지위원회) is reviewing a government version of the Digital Healthcare Act (디지털헬스케어법) put forward by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (보건복지부). The Bill on the Use of Biodata and the Promotion of AI-Bio Research and Development (바이오데이터 활용 및 인공지능바이오 연구개발 촉진에 관한 법률안, hereafter the biodata bill), introduced by lawmakers in December 2025, had not passed as of 22 November and is waiting for review by the science committee's bill review subcommittee. If it passes, research institutions and researchers could use pseudonymized portions of genetic and clinical data for AI biotech research without the consent of the people the data describe.

Supporters of the bill argue that getting access to data takes too long. Reporting on a forum held to push for the law, the Korean daily Electronic Times (전자신문) wrote that data from rare-disease patients can take up to 18 months to reach researchers. Six medical, patient and social welfare groups are concerned about use without consent and about a single consolidated review, and on 18 November they published a statement calling for both biodata bills to be scrapped.

Under the biodata bill, the Minister of Science and ICT, who heads the Ministry of Science and ICT (과학기술정보통신부, MSIT), could build a national integrated biodata system and request data from public bodies such as the National Health Insurance Service (국민건강보험공단). The data must be pseudonymized before they are handed over, meaning fields such as names are replaced with codes, which differs from anonymization, where the person can no longer be identified at all. Article 23 lists what may be used without consent, including partial genetic information that cannot identify an individual on its own, and clinical, environmental and other data about unspecified individuals or groups. A presidential decree (an executive order) can add further items, and data that could easily be combined with other information to identify someone are excluded.

Article 21 sets up a research review committee to carry out a single consolidated review. It has up to 20 members appointed or commissioned by the Minister of Science and ICT, including people recommended by the Minister of Health and Welfare and by the Chair of South Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission (개인정보보호위원회, PIPC, the country's data protection authority). Matters this committee has reviewed are deemed to have undergone the review required by the Bioethics and Safety Act (생명윤리 및 안전에 관한 법률), that is, the research ethics review carried out by each institution's institutional review board (IRB). Articles 21 and 23 appear only in the bill introduced in December 2025, and according to the statement, the other bill removes these provisions.

Article 4 provides that, except where other laws contain special provisions, the use of biodata and AI-bio research and development are governed by this Act. According to the statement, the PIPC is concerned that if the Act were applied ahead of the Personal Information Protection Act (개인정보 보호법), the existing system of protections would be weakened.

In the statement, the groups also criticize the government's bill for using pseudonymized data without consent, but argue that health data should at least be debated within the legal framework for health and medical care. Medigate News, a Korean medical news outlet, reported that the health ministry and the Democratic Party of Korea aim to pass the government bill by the end of 2026.

## Perspective {#perspective}

Pseudonymization removes the fields that identify someone directly, but each person's records stay linked to one another. Research needs that linkage, and the risk of re-identification comes from the same place. In October, the Korean outlet NoCut News reported that a health ministry official, speaking about the biodata bill, said it is hard to claim that pseudonymization alone removes every identification risk.

The Electronic Times reported on 19 October that the health ministry takes a cautious position on gathering treatment information in one place, and on the overlap with the functions of existing IRBs and data review boards (DRBs, committees that decide whether data can be released). According to the same report, MSIT considers legislation necessary to promote AI research and plans to keep talking with the health ministry.

Article 23 of the biodata bill only sets out what may be used, and the debate over opt-out (data are included by default, and individuals can ask to be excluded) has focused on the government bill. In a July 2025 ruling, the Supreme Court (대법원) held that individuals cannot demand a halt to pseudonymization carried out for scientific research, statistics or archiving in the public interest. Citing that ruling, the health ministry explained that the government bill follows the framework of the existing Personal Information Protection Act, while separately requiring consent for information with a high risk of re-identification, such as data on rare diseases.

At a National Assembly forum on the government bill on 2 November, a researcher from a public health research group argued that opt-out should be guaranteed, and a division director at the health ministry said an opt-out mechanism needed to be considered. At a public hearing on the Digital Healthcare Act, a representative of the Korean Medical Association (대한의사협회) also noted that once medical data are used to train AI, they are very hard to take back.

Under the biodata bill, the Article 21 committee carries out a single consolidated review, and according to the statement, the health ministry opposed this on the grounds that it could undermine bioethics safeguards. Under the government bill, review is handled by each agency's own review committee, whose members, according to the health ministry's explanation, include representatives of the people whose data are used and outside members. An Electronic Times reporter wrote that research is delayed because researchers must apply separately for an IRB exemption and for DRB review, and both committees take a conservative line and repeatedly ask for more documents.

According to its official documentation, the UK's OpenSAFELY keeps data with the organizations that already hold it. Researchers send their analysis code into a secure zone there to run, and results are released only after review. The biodata bill instead gathers the data into the integrated system first.

Both biodata bills are still under review, and readers do not need to do anything for now. Developments to watch are when the science committee's bill review subcommittee takes up the bills, and whether the government bill passes by the end of 2026.

In Taiwan, the Constitutional Court's Judgment 111-Hsien-Pan-13 of 2022 concerned the National Health Insurance Administration providing National Health Insurance data to government agencies and academic research institutions for uses beyond the original purpose. The court held that the lack of any way for people to ask for this to stop violated Article 22 of the Constitution, and gave the authorities three years to enact or amend legislation. The National Health Insurance Data Management Act (全民健康保險資料管理條例), promulgated in December 2025, provides for requests to stop such uses of all or part of a person's data, and leaves the date it takes effect to the Executive Yuan. A stop does not apply retroactively, so uses that took place before the request are unaffected.

According to Japan's Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC), the country's Act on the Protection of Personal Information requires consent, with exceptions, both to acquire sensitive information such as medical history and to provide personal data to third parties. An amendment promulgated on 17 July 2026, to take effect within two years of promulgation, removes the requirement for individual consent for providing personal data to third parties, and for acquiring sensitive information that has already been made public, when the data are used only to produce statistics, which in the PPC's explanation includes AI development that can be treated as producing statistics. Both parties must publish what they will do and agree in writing to use the data only for that purpose, and they are barred from using the data for anything else or passing them on. In our reading, once in force the amendment would, like the Korean biodata bill, allow use without individual consent, but only for producing statistics and under a written agreement between the parties, whereas Taiwan's Act lets individuals ask for use beyond the original purpose to stop.

When comparing systems in different places, readers can ask who can get the data, whether the data are centralized, whether individuals can refuse, and what happens to data that have already been used. They can also ask who appoints the reviewers, and whether the system can shorten the time researchers wait for data.
