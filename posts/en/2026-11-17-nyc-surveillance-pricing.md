The New York City Council voted 44 to 5 on 12 November to pass a bill that, with some exceptions, bars businesses from using algorithms and personal data to charge different customers different prices for the same goods or services. It applies to businesses domiciled in the city or transacting with consumers there, so readers elsewhere are not directly affected by this city bill. As of 14 November, the council's legislative system still listed the bill as awaiting the mayor's signature, and the text takes effect one year after it becomes law.

The bill calls the practice it bans surveillance pricing, while a draft from the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and documents from industry groups call it personalized pricing. The text defines it as pricing set completely or in part by an algorithm that uses personal data to offer different prices to different customers for the same goods or services. Personal data under the bill means any data that identifies or could reasonably be linked, directly or indirectly, with a specific consumer or device. Location data that consumers provide themselves and that is used to calculate trip and delivery fees or to assess local supply and demand does not count.

The bill also permits several kinds of what it calls bona fide custom discounts. Loyalty or rewards programs that consumers opt into can tier members by past purchase history, provided that history is not combined or cross-referenced with other personal data. Status such as veteran, senior or teacher, or a birthday, must be offered by the consumer rather than inferred by the business, and both kinds of discount must be clearly disclosed. Insurance, pricing that assesses creditworthiness based on credit reports, and pricing required or expressly authorized by federal or state law fall outside the ban.

Enforcement falls to the city's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP), with civil penalties of up to $1,000 per violation. Under the text, a business with fewer than 15 locations nationwide that operates mainly in physical locations in New York City can, after a first violation, correct it and issue full refunds within 30 days of being notified. Businesses that offer custom discounts or use surveillance pricing must also set up a procedure that lets consumers who shop in the city request what personal data was considered in setting a price and how it was considered.

On 19 August the FTC proposed a draft enforcement policy statement on personalized pricing (Proposed Enforcement Policy Statement), which sets out how it intends to enforce existing law and is not a new rule in itself. According to the draft, Congress has not given the FTC authority to prohibit personalized pricing in all circumstances, and the FTC intends to enforce Section 5 of the FTC Act, which prohibits unfair or deceptive practices in commerce, aggressively. Where consumers reasonably expect that prices will not vary based on their personal data, businesses should clearly disclose that the price is personalized, the basis for the personalization and the types of data used, and failing to do so is likely to violate Section 5. The comment period closed in September, and as of 14 November we had found no final version.

The draft includes several examples, which the FTC marks as non-exhaustive and offered only for discussion. They include a food delivery company quoting higher prices to customers whose data suggests they are unlikely or unable to leave home, a grocery chain charging a delivery customer more for milk because several children live in the household, and a rideshare company charging more for a ride to a medical facility when data suggests the user has a medical emergency. Separately, The Record reported that grocery stores, ride-sharing companies and airlines are thought to be widely using this kind of pricing, though public evidence is limited.

## Perspective {#perspective}

As the FTC's draft describes it, businesses analyze personal data to estimate how much an individual consumer is willing to pay and whether they are likely to comparison shop, then set prices accordingly. One investigation cited in the council's press release found that a major grocery delivery platform's pricing experiments produced price differences between individuals as high as 23 percent. In another cited in the release, the price of a television rose from $499.99 to $599.99 after the customer pulled into the store's parking lot. According to the release, consumers find this kind of pricing hard to detect.

Under New York City's bill, businesses cannot price based on personal data even if they tell customers in advance. The exceptions listed in the text are the discounts described above, plus insurance, credit reports and pricing required or authorized by law. A Consumer Reports policy analyst who worked on the campaign for the bill told The Record that once the law takes effect, eight million people in New York City will no longer have to guess whether their search history or where their cursor hovers on a page is shaping the price they see.

The FTC's draft relies on disclosure instead, and in a footnote the FTC declines for now to take a position on whether some personalized pricing is still unfair even when fully disclosed. The draft's own example is that telling consumers only that a price is "exclusive" is likely misleading, while explaining that it was estimated from purchase history on the same logged-in account may be sufficient.

The Future of Privacy Forum (FPF), which describes itself as a non-profit organization advancing data protection and digital governance practices, filed comments in September. It asked the FTC to clarify whether all undisclosed personalized pricing counts as deceptive or only pricing that leads to higher prices, and how it would judge whether a price is higher. FPF wrote that many retailers personalize prices to offer some consumers lower prices, through loyalty programs, personalized coupons and discounts for students and seniors, and recommended comparing against a retailer's real internal baseline price. It also cited limited space and user-experience considerations as obstacles to in-depth pricing disclosures and asked the FTC for guidance on putting the disclosure requirement into practice.

According to City & State, the bill has faced opposition from businesses whose services fall under it, including Uber, though we could not find a public statement from Uber itself. In the same report, the council Speaker rejected claims that the bill would stop businesses from offering some discounts, saying they can still give discounts but cannot mine personal data to charge different prices for the same product. NACS, a trade association for convenience and fuel retailers, asked the FTC in August to extend the comment deadline, and its notice said the issue is rife with misinformation and poses significant risks to longstanding retail pricing practices, loyalty programs and discounts.

In the draft, the FTC cites limited economic research, including a 2023 study, indicating that personalized pricing is likely to increase business profits while benefiting some consumers and harming others, and that more sophisticated methods are less likely to benefit consumers. In comments marked as representing only the author's views, American Action Forum's director of competition policy cites the same 2023 study, which found that over 60 percent of consumers benefited from personalization even when overall consumer surplus (the gap between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay) declined. The author argues that a consumer-expectations standard could chill pricing innovation and competition and lead to subjective enforcement, and urges the FTC to focus Section 5 enforcement on clear fraud and misrepresentation, such as claiming everyone pays the same price while personalizing it with customer data.

Explaining why disclosure matters, the FTC writes in the draft that consumers who do not know a price is personalized cannot use a VPN or private browsing, switch businesses, or walk away to avoid higher prices. According to American Action Forum's comments, once prices are disclosed consumers may try to hide information that signals they are willing to pay more. Neither document says how much of the price difference these measures can avoid.

Readers outside New York City do not need to do anything now, and consumers in the city will have to wait until the law takes effect before they can ask businesses which personal data went into a price and how. Even then, insurance, pricing that assesses creditworthiness based on credit reports, and pricing required or authorized by law remain outside the ban. We infer that purchase history and loyalty data built up under a logged-in account do not disappear when someone switches to a VPN or private browsing. As of 14 November, the legislative system showed no mayoral signature, and the FTC's draft had not been finalized.

According to The Record and City & State, the mayor supports the bill. Developments to watch are whether the mayor signs it and whether the FTC's final version changes the disclosure requirements. The Record also cited Consumer Reports as saying that the New York State Legislature passed a similar state bill in June, which the governor had still not signed as of the report on 13 November.

In mainland China, three sets of rules address this kind of pricing, starting with Article 24 of the Personal Information Protection Law, in force since 1 November 2021, requires automated decisions made with personal information to be transparent and fair, and bars unreasonable differential treatment of individuals in transaction terms such as price. Article 21 of the algorithmic recommendation provisions issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), in force since 1 March 2022, bars recommendation providers selling to consumers from using algorithms to apply unreasonable differential treatment in price based on characteristics such as preferences or transaction habits. The Rules on Price Conduct of Internet Platforms, issued by the National Development and Reform Commission, the State Administration for Market Regulation and the CAC and in force since 10 April 2026 with a five-year term. Article 15 bars platforms and their merchants from using data, algorithms or platform rules to set different prices for the same product under the same terms based on information such as willingness to pay, ability to pay, preferences or habits "without the consumer's knowledge" (our translation).

In our reading, tying the prohibition to whether consumers know may put Article 15 closer to the FTC's emphasis on disclosure than to New York City's bill, which applies even when a business gives notice. We could not confirm whether Article 15 still applies once a consumer knows, and the other two rules leave open what counts as unreasonable. When we checked on 14 November, we found no rule or public statement from authorities in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, Japan or South Korea that specifically addresses pricing based on personal data, which does not mean none exists.

When comparing New York City's approach with the FTC's, readers can ask on the privacy side which personal data may be used to set prices, and whether consumers can learn and look up which data a business used. On effectiveness, they can ask whether an outright ban or a disclosure requirement does more to let consumers avoid markups based on personal data while keeping discounts such as loyalty and student pricing. With public evidence limited, another question is whether regulators can detect personalized pricing at all. Since, according to its draft, the FTC lacks authority for a blanket ban, readers can also ask how the tools available to cities, states and the federal government differ.
