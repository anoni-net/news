Norwegian police announced two investigations into Telenor ASA on 15 September, with charges in both. Kripos, the national criminal investigation service, charged the company with complicity in crimes against humanity over allegations that its Myanmar subsidiary repeatedly handed customers' historical traffic data to the military regime between the February 2021 coup and the sale of the unit in March 2022. The security police, PST, charged it with breaching sanctions law because the sale included sanctioned surveillance equipment without permission from the foreign ministry. Both cases concern the Myanmar subsidiary, and the people affected were its customers at the time.

Telenor's statement said its employees risked imprisonment, torture or the death penalty if they disobeyed the military, and that it had no real choice. No individuals have been charged. According to Access Now, the charges follow a police complaint filed in 2025 by Justice For Myanmar and ICJ Norway. Separately, on 17 September a Norwegian district court allowed a class action covering at least 1,253 people to proceed, and NRK has reported that Telenor disclosed sensitive metadata on more than 1,300 customers, including call logs, names, addresses and last known locations.

## Perspective {#perspective}

What makes telecom data dangerous is often not the content of calls but the records around them: who contacted whom, when, and from where. That metadata maps a person's network and movements without any wiretap, and in the hands of a junta after a coup it becomes a way to find people. The same link appears in Kripos's statement: the data was handed over at the same time as the regime was responsible for extensive abuses against parts of the civilian population.

Access Now's statement says that leaving a market does not mean leaving human rights responsibilities behind, and that due diligence has to cover how a company exits as well as how it operates. The two Norwegian charges line up with that: one concerns handing over data while operating, the other the surveillance equipment transferred on the way out. Telenor sold the unit to Lebanon's M1 Group, whose local partner Shwe Byain Phyu later took a majority stake; Justice For Myanmar has reported on that company's military links and on a German-made lawful interception gateway that changed hands with the deal.

How much traffic data reveals depends on how tightly numbers are tied to identities, and across Asia that link is often mandatory. Mainland China has required carriers to check ID and record real identities for phone lines since 2013. Hong Kong made real-name registration compulsory for all SIM cards, and unregistered prepaid cards stopped working after 23 February 2023. Myanmar's junta went further in March 2026 with a central equipment identity register: according to The Irrawaddy, every device's IMEI must now be registered before it can connect, so switching SIM cards no longer protects anyone.

Users cannot control records held by their carrier. People who need to protect their contacts can move sensitive conversations to end-to-end encrypted messengers, and should assume that calls and SMS records are visible to the operator and can be handed over.
