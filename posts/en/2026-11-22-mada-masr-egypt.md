According to Al Jazeera, the New Cairo Misdemeanour Court in Egypt convicted the editor-in-chief of the independent news site Mada Masr on 27 October of running a news website without a licence, and imposed the maximum fine allowed by law, 3 million Egyptian pounds. The court also ordered the site closed and the outlet's equipment confiscated. A hearing in a separate case against the editor-in-chief was set for 11 November, and as of 15 November we could not find its outcome. The ruling was made in Egypt, and readers elsewhere are not directly affected.

An article by the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) notes that Mada Masr has published only online since it was founded in 2013. EFF, Reporters Without Borders (RSF) and The National all report that the site has been blocked inside Egypt since 2017. According to a report at the time by the Committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ), the Supreme Council for Media Regulation (SCMR), which handles media licences, announced a further six-month block of the site in October 2023.

According to The National, the Supreme State Security Prosecution questioned the editor-in-chief for more than six hours on 21 October about a report Mada Masr had published, and she was released on bail of 100,000 Egyptian pounds. The next day prosecutors sent her to trial on the licensing charge, and EFF writes that the proceedings took a single hearing. Al Jazeera reports that she was taken into custody after the ruling and released several hours later once Mada Masr paid the fine in full, while EFF's article and a joint statement by several organisations put her detention at seven hours. The National also counts this as the sixth case against Mada Masr or its journalists in six years, and EFF's article cites CPJ's count of at least 25 journalists imprisoned in Egypt.

According to a Mada Masr editorial quoted by Al Jazeera, the court refused the defence's requests to examine the case file, present evidence and call witnesses. The editorial also says the judge appointed a separate lawyer to defend the editor-in-chief, although all eight of her lawyers were present and she objected. That joint statement, issued on 28 October by the Tahrir Institute for Middle East Policy (TIMEP) and other organisations, says Mada Masr's legal team plans to appeal. Al Jazeera also reports that the head of the Egyptian Journalists' Syndicate said the union would take legal action.

EFF's article of 2 November describes the 11 November hearing as the second trial in two weeks, on a charge of spreading false news. Al Jazeera and the joint statement describe it as a hearing to review a 2024 conviction in absentia on charges of publishing false news and running an unlicensed website. According to both, the editor-in-chief learned while being processed for release on 27 October that she had been sentenced in absentia to one year in prison in 2024. Al Jazeera, citing Mada Masr, reports that her lawyers challenged that ruling and the court set a hearing for 11 November, which Al Jazeera calls a retrial.

## Perspective {#perspective}

Al Jazeera reported no immediate official comment on the 27 October ruling. As of 15 November we could not find any public statement on the ruling from the Egyptian government or the SCMR, and the only accounts available come from Mada Masr and advocacy groups.

According to Al Jazeera, the ruling rests on Egypt's 2018 media law, which sets a fine of 1 million to 3 million Egyptian pounds for running an unlicensed website, along with closure of the site and confiscation of equipment. The same report says an administrative court in 2023 upheld the SCMR's rejection of Mada Masr's licence application, on the grounds that there was no proof the outlet's trademark had been registered. Mada Masr's account is that its applications in 2018 and 2020 went unanswered and that it learned of the rejection only from prosecutors during another case in 2022, and its lawyer said he had submitted a trademark registration certificate.

EFF's article argues that requiring news organisations to obtain government authorisation to operate creates a mechanism for restricting who gets to publish. The joint statement from TIMEP and other organisations, whose signatories include Access Now, Amnesty International and ARTICLE 19, calls on the government to grant licences to independent outlets without discrimination and to stop using the licensing provisions of the media law to criminalise journalism. A new application Mada Masr filed in June 2023 was still pending when Al Jazeera reported on the ruling.

According to CPJ's report, the SCMR gave two reasons for the 2023 block, "practicing media activities without a license" and "publishing false news without checking its sources," and also referred that case to the prosecutor general. We could not find any public information on the basis of the charges in the 2024 conviction in absentia. On false news charges, EFF argues that governments should not decide which reporting may be published, especially when the reporting concerns their own conduct.

OONI (Open Observatory of Network Interference) publishes censorship measurements run by volunteers in many countries. Each time a probe tests a website, a control connection is also made from an uncensored network, and the measurement is flagged as an anomaly when the two results differ. OONI's public API shows 33 measurements of `madamasr.com` recorded as Egypt between 16 October and 15 November (we queried on 15 November), of which 22 were flagged as anomalies, 2 failed and none was confirmed as blocked. Of the remaining 9 measurements with no anomaly, 6 came from Cloudflare's network rather than an ordinary Egyptian internet provider, so they do not reflect what typical users see.

In the two anomalous measurements we sampled, the Domain Name System (DNS) lookup matched the control, and the anomaly was an HTTP connection reset, meaning the connection was cut off after it was set up and the page did not load. Anomalous measurements have appeared every month since November 2025, with only 3 or 4 a month from July to September and 15 in October. The number of measurements is small and OONI only flags them as anomalies, so these data alone cannot show whether the site is still blocked in Egypt. Nor do the measurements show whether or how the 27 October closure order has been carried out (our inference).

Readers outside Egypt do not need to change any settings. Anyone who wants to check whether a site can be reached in different places can look up its public measurement records by URL in OONI Explorer. Developments worth following are the outcome of the 11 November hearing, the appeal against the 27 October ruling, and whether the site is actually closed under the order.

In mainland China, Article 5 of the Provisions on the Administration of Internet News Information Services, in force since June 2017, requires a permit to provide internet news information services through websites, apps, social media accounts and other channels. Under Article 22, providing such services without a permit leads to an order to stop and a fine of 10,000 to 30,000 yuan, according to the text published on the Cyberspace Administration of China's website (checked on 15 November). In Myanmar, CPJ reported, citing news reports, that the state broadcaster MRTV announced on 8 March 2021 that the military government had revoked the operating licences of five independent outlets, which were general operating licences rather than a permit specific to online news. CPJ also cited The New York Times as reporting that losing the licences could make reporting illegal for the five.

When comparing how different places regulate online media, one can ask whether licence decisions have a deadline, whether rejections come with reasons, whether applicants have a remedy, and whether operating without a licence is punished with a fine or with closure. Other questions are whether the authorities have published what the licensing regime and the false news offences are meant to achieve, and how much of that has been achieved since they took effect.
