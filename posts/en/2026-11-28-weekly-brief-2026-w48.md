Week 48 brings together six stories from reports and announcements dated between 14 and 22 November, and the one on France starts with what happened in October.

## The uBlock Origin element picker vulnerability {#ublock-origin-element-picker}

According to the project's security advisory on GitHub, uBlock Origin, a browser extension that blocks ads and trackers, had a high-severity vulnerability in version 1.75.2 and earlier, which was fixed in version 1.75.4, released on 3 November. The researcher who reported it published the details on their blog on 15 November, explaining that a malicious website could trick the extension into adding arbitrary trusted filters. According to the researcher, such filters can inject JavaScript into web pages, so an attacker could run their own code on any website. The researcher wrote that all it takes to trigger the attack is for the website to get the user to click once on the page.

The version the researcher actually tested was the one for Firefox built on the older Manifest V2 (MV2) specification, and the researcher mentioned a similar but less problematic issue in uBO Lite, which uses the newer MV3 specification. The release notes for 1.77.0, published on 22 November, differ: they state that none of the reported security issues affect uBO Lite. The same notes also list a second vulnerability, reported by another researcher and fixed in 1.76.2, and in both cases the user had to visit a web page controlled by an attacker first.

In the 1.77.0 release notes, users are advised to confirm that every filter list they subscribe to is one they added themselves, and that every custom filter is one they created. Anyone who has turned on the "Allow custom filters requiring trust" option should check their trusted filters in particular.

## A double free vulnerability in GPGME {#gpgme-double-free}

GnuPG is an open-source tool for OpenPGP encryption and signing, and GPGME is the library that lets programs such as mail clients and clients for the XMPP instant messaging protocol call on GnuPG's functions. According to a security advisory the GnuPG developers sent on 20 November, all publicly released versions before 2.3.1 contain a critical vulnerability. With an OpenPGP key that has a crafted, very long user ID, an attacker can trigger a double free (a bug where the same block of memory is freed twice) and steal data from applications, for example by sending signed data. The advisory adds that using the bug for remote code execution cannot be ruled out.

The software using GPGME named in the advisory includes Gpg4win, an encryption suite for Windows, most mail clients, many XMPP clients and the file-sharing software Samba. The advisory recommends updating immediately, and Gpg4win users can move to version 5.1.2, which includes the fixed GPGME. People who maintain packages need to update the GPGME shared library to 2.3.1, and programs that link GPGME statically need to be rebuilt against 2.3.1.

## Malware injected into Optimizely customers' websites {#optimizely-malware}

People who visited some large websites on 16 November may have had their browsers load malware that steals payment card details and cryptocurrency. According to an article published that day by the security company Report URI, the websites of 178 organisations served malicious JavaScript to their visitors through Optimizely's Web Experimentation service, which lets a website show different layouts to different visitors as a test. The list Report URI published includes Zoom, the BBC, CNN and IKEA, as well as HSBC's Taiwan website, `www.hsbc.com.tw`, and the real number of affected organisations is likely higher. The malware swapped cryptocurrency wallet addresses for the attacker's, skimmed payment cards and stole cookies and screenshots, and on iPhones and iPads it also delivered two iOS exploit kits.

The attacker modified the Project JavaScript in customer projects, which is code that customers write themselves in the Optimizely dashboard and that runs on every page of their site. According to Optimizely's account as reported by CMSWire on 18 November, the attacker exploited a flaw in the Web Experimentation account signup flow to impersonate users in targeted customer accounts. The attacker then inserted malicious code into those customers' projects. According to the same account, Optimizely closed the vulnerability within three hours of detecting it, and fewer than 5% of its customers were affected.

Report URI observed that by 23:11 UTC on 16 November (07:11 Taipei time on 17 November), all the infected files had been cleaned. Report URI advised anyone who paid by card on an affected site to watch their card statements. According to Report URI's analysis, card numbers entered in fields hosted by payment services such as Stripe and Adyen were out of the skimmer's reach, while card numbers typed directly into a site's own forms were not. Comments in the exploit kit's own code list iOS 18.7.3 and later and 26.3 and later as patched, so Report URI recommends updating iOS 18 to 18.7.3 or later and iOS 26 to 26.3 or later.

## The USB vulnerability fixed in SecureDrop Workstation 1.9.1 {#securedrop-workstation-usb}

SecureDrop is a system that news organisations use to receive documents submitted by sources, and SecureDrop Workstation is the workstation journalists use to handle those documents, built on Qubes OS, an operating system that isolates different tasks in separate virtual machines. According to SecureDrop's advisory on 19 November, version 1.9.1, released on 17 November, fixes a high-severity vulnerability in the code that attaches USB devices automatically. The workstation isolates USB and other devices in a separate qube (a virtual machine in Qubes OS), and when a USB stick is plugged in, it is attached automatically to `sd-devices`, the qube used for exporting documents, while a printer is attached to `sd-printers`. A flaw in the permissions policy meant that either of these qubes, once compromised, could gain access to any connected device, such as a webcam or a Bluetooth adapter, provided the attacker had first gained the ability to run code inside the qube.

Both qubes are configured as offline, disposable qubes, which limits an attacker's ability to exfiltrate data and makes exported or printed documents harder to reach after a reboot. Gaining access to devices could still give an attacker some form of network access or persistence. The project is not aware of any exploitation. The advisory asks administrators to check whether a webcam, microphone or other peripherals appear in the Qubes devices menu and, if so, to disable whichever of those devices they can in the laptop's BIOS settings.

## The Italian data protection authority's fine on Meta {#garante-meta-camera-roll}

The Android version of the Facebook app has a camera roll suggestions feature ("Get camera roll suggestions when you're browsing Facebook") that picks photos and videos from the phone's camera roll and suggests them to users as material for posts. In a press release on 14 November, the Garante, Italy's data protection authority, announced a fine of €805 million against Meta over this feature. The grounds were that Meta used the feature to access photos, videos and related metadata on Italian users' phones without obtaining specific consent and without adequately informing them. The investigation found that a single permission for camera roll access covered several features across the whole app, that the prompt shown before granting it said only "enable other features", and that the feature itself was turned on by default.

Under the Garante's interpretation of Article 122 of the Italian Data Protection Code (Codice privacy), any access to information stored on a phone requires free and informed consent, whether or not any data is transmitted. The Garante ordered Meta to make camera roll suggestions off by default within 60 days, including for users who have already granted permission, and to obtain separate consent for this feature. In setting the fine, the Garante considered the number of people affected, about 20 million Android users in Italy. A new feature launched in 2026, which processes camera roll content in the cloud, falls outside this decision, and the Garante has reserved the right to investigate it separately.

According to the Italian outlet L'Unione Sarda, a Meta spokesperson said the company strongly disagrees with the Garante's assessment of the facts and its application of the law, and Meta announced that it would appeal. The spokesperson called the fine disproportionate, and said the feature requires prior permission on the device and can be changed in the settings at any time.

## France's idea of DNS filtering for social media platforms, and the amendment extending Pharos {#france-dns-pharos}

French media reported that in October the government had considered using DNS filtering to block social media platforms in specific areas during protests, a plan that was never carried out. DNS filtering stops a domain name from resolving to its address, so it can only block an entire service and cannot remove individual posts or accounts. According to a CircleID report on 26 October, citing La Tribune's reporting on documents obtained by the investigative outlet Blast, the Prime Minister's office sent a confidential letter on 22 October asking four telecom operators to be ready. If the demonstrations on 24 October turned violent, the government wanted the operators to be able to temporarily restrict TikTok, Instagram and Snapchat on 4G and 5G networks in specific areas, and X was later added to the list.

According to the same report, at a meeting on 23 October the operators questioned whether this was technically feasible, and pointed out that switching to a VPN or another DNS resolver would get around this kind of filtering. According to a CircleID report on 18 November, the digital rights organisation ARTICLE 19 urged the French authorities in a statement on 17 November to abandon the idea of broad platform blocking, warning that such restrictions could disrupt lawful communications without effectively preventing access to the targeted content.

According to reports by CircleID on 26 October and Public Sénat on 28 October, the government submitted Amendment No. 94 on 26 October to the "casseurs-payeurs" bill (literally "vandals pay"), which the Senate was then debating. The amendment would extend Pharos, the mechanism the administrative authorities use to demand the removal of online content, to content on social networks that incites attacks on people's lives, theft or vandalism and is likely to seriously disrupt public order. Under the amendment, the authorities could require hosting providers and publishers to remove such content within 24 hours, and the amendment passed with the support of the Senate majority and several other political groups.

In the chamber, the interior minister said the amendment used Pharos to remove content and did not aim to shut down social networks, while left-wing senators denounced it as an authoritarian drift (dérive autoritaire). As of CircleID's 18 November report, the bill still had to go through further parliamentary stages.
