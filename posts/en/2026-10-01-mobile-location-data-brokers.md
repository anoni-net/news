On 16 September The Markup and Consumer Reports published what volunteers found when they used new US state privacy laws to request their files from the largest data brokers. The reports held names, addresses and Social Security numbers, plus more than a thousand guesses about each person's traits and habits. The same month, the Freedom of the Press Foundation (FPF) set out two settings that reduce the location data a phone gives away. The settings apply to iPhone and Android anywhere, not only in the US.

By its own account, Acxiom has profiles on roughly 95% of US adults and sorts people into more than 3,000 categories, from the ages of their children to estimated "alcohol usage". Laws in states such as Minnesota and Oregon require disclosure of who bought the data, and the Acxiom files reviewed listed more than 100 buyers, including insurers, banks, drug makers, carmakers and Disney. Brokers can hold a phone's advertising ID, a smart TV's tracking identifier, a home IP address and a car's VIN, and location data can show visits to a military base, a school or a medical specialist.

## Perspective {#perspective}

According to The Markup, opting out of one broker removes your data from that broker only, not from the copies already sold. That makes the right to see what companies hold the more useful tool, and it exists across much of Asia. Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act, Article 10, requires organisations to answer inquiries, allow review or provide copies on request, with a decision due within 15 days under Article 13. Hong Kong's Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance, section 18, lets individuals request a copy of their data, which must be supplied within 40 days. Mainland China's Personal Information Protection Law, Article 45, gives individuals the right to consult and copy their information.

Singapore goes a step further in the direction the US buyer lists point. Section 21 of its Personal Data Protection Act requires organisations, on request, to provide not only the personal data they hold but also information about how it has been used or disclosed within the past year. For readers anywhere, a request shows only what one company holds.

The advertising ID is what lets records from different apps be tied to one phone, which is why FPF starts there. According to FPF, location tracking often runs through real-time bidding on ads and software kits embedded in apps, linked back to devices by that ID. FPF's post also cites Pentagon officials who have said ad-based location tracking on troops' phones and computers has been disabled. On iPhone, go to Settings > Privacy & security > Tracking and turn off Allow apps to request to track; on Android, Settings > Security & privacy > Privacy controls > Ads > Delete advertising ID. The second step is to switch off location access for every app that does not truly need it, such as anything other than maps, and turn it back on when needed. Google's help page puts the Android setting under Settings > Google > All services > Ads, and its location can vary by phone maker. Neither step requires installing anything, and both take a few minutes.
