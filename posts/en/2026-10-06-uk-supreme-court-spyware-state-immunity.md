On 27 July the UK Supreme Court dismissed Bahrain's appeal by a 3–2 majority, so two claimants living in the UK can keep suing the Bahraini government. The claimants allege that from around September 2011 Bahrain infected their computers with FinSpy spyware, and that learning of it caused them psychiatric injury. The ruling decides only whether UK courts can hear the case. Bahrain denies the allegations, whether any hacking took place is left for trial, and courts outside the UK apply their own law.

According to the judgment, the State Immunity Act 1978 grants foreign states immunity, but section 5 excepts death, personal injury or damage to tangible property "caused by an act or omission in the United Kingdom". Article 11 of the European Convention on State Immunity, which the UK has ratified, also requires the author of the injury to be present, and both sides agree this case fails that test. On the assumed facts, the operators were likely abroad, using a command and control server in Bahrain, while the claimants and their computers were in the UK.

The majority held that the absence of a presence requirement in section 5 was a deliberate departure from the Convention, and that an "act" includes one done through a device or by remote means. Remotely manipulating a UK computer from abroad is therefore an act in the UK.

Filed in 2020, the claim survived Bahrain's immunity plea in the High Court in 2023 and the Court of Appeal in 2024. The claimants' law firm, Leigh Day, calls the ruling a foundation for its other spyware claims in its press release. As of 29 September we found no public statement from the Bahraini government on the ruling.

## Perspective {#perspective}

In the judgment, Bahrain argued that the harm was caused by commands entered abroad, with file corruption or data copying on the claimants' computers as secondary events. One dissenting justice likens this to firing a rifle across a border river, placing the act in Bahrain where commands are entered and treating a camera switching on in the UK as its effect. The claimants list ten classes of acts in the UK, including transmitting installation files, overwriting the Master Boot Record, activating microphones and cameras, and logging keystrokes, which the majority found together amount to surveillance in the UK.

In a Lawfare article, legal researchers from Citizen Lab and Access Now argue that the dissent's reading would deny immunity to a state that sends agents in to kill, yet grant it to one using a drone flown from abroad. The majority found that the Convention lets the UK widen the exception and that state practice supports applying it to sovereign acts. Bahrain argued this reading puts the UK in breach of the Convention, and the dissent found customary international law allows the exception only where the author is present.

Asian laws split on the same question of presence. Section 7 of Singapore's State Immunity Act 1979 copies the UK wording, "caused by an act or omission in Singapore", with no presence requirement. Japan's 2009 Act on the Civil Jurisdiction of Japan with respect to a Foreign State goes the other way, and its Article 10 requires the person who performed the act to have been in Japan at the time.

Mainland China's Foreign State Immunity Law, in force since 1 January 2024, lifts immunity under Article 9 for compensation claims over injury, death or property loss caused by a foreign state's relevant acts within Chinese territory. Like the UK and Singapore, it states no presence requirement, and Article 21 adds reciprocity for states that grant China less immunity.

The court's case page shows that this appeal dealt only with immunity, on the claimants' pleaded facts. Per the Lawfare article, the claimants must prove attribution to Bahrain, causation and injury back in the High Court. Section 13 of the UK Act also bars enforcing a judgment against a foreign state's property unless it is in commercial use or the state consents in writing.

Section 5 covers only death, personal injury and damage to tangible property, so claims over data exposure or economic loss alone fall outside it. In another UK case described in the article, expert forensic evidence showed the claimant's phone had been hacked with Pegasus.
