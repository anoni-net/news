On 13 November, Hong Kong's Office of the Privacy Commissioner for Personal Data (PCPD, the statutory body responsible for personal data protection) said it had begun a compliance check, on its own initiative, into the facial recognition access system at Laguna City. In a compliance check, the PCPD asks an organisation for information to see whether it is complying with the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Laguna City is a private housing estate (the Hong Kong term for a large residential development) in Lam Tin, and according to the PCPD's statement more than 8,000 households may be involved. Laguna City residents are directly affected, other Hong Kong estates that bring in similar systems are bound by the same ordinance, and places outside Hong Kong are not affected.

Laguna City has four phases, and the property management company is installing facial recognition gates in phases 1, 2 and 4. A Sing Tao Headline reporter at the estate on 13 November saw installed gates still covered with paper. According to Dot Dot News, which relayed the company's response to the media, the gates are meant to make coming and going easier for residents and to improve management services, and before launch the company will hold tea gatherings and issue notices to introduce the system to residents. The company also said the personal data it collects will be handled in accordance with the ordinance, and Hong Kong Free Press (HKFP) reported that a Kwun Tong district councillor said on an RTHK programme that the system is due to go live in January.

HKFP also reported that the councillor had heard views both for and against, with some elderly residents finding the new system more convenient. Of the residents Sing Tao Headline interviewed, some said they would not use it because they worried about data leaks, and one found tapping a door card quicker and easier than facial recognition. Another planned to wait and see, and to try it once it is in use.

According to the PCPD's statement as quoted by HKFP, "facial images constitute sensitive personal data", and on that basis the PCPD asked the property management company for further information. According to the statement, the check is to make sure the company follows the ordinance and its data protection principles in collecting, holding, processing and using biometric data, in particular the requirements on data security. The data protection principles are the ordinance's basic rules on how personal data is collected, kept, used and protected. HKFP reported that the company told the media on 16 November that it would cooperate with the check.

## Perspective {#perspective}

The property management company gave convenience as its reason for the gates, and some elderly residents see it the same way. The PCPD's Guidance on Collection and Use of Biometric Data, first published in 2015 and revised in 2020, lists facial images among physiological data, most of which cannot be changed. Among the consequences of wrongful disclosure of biometric data set out in the guidance are re-identification, impersonation and even discrimination. Its recommendations for reducing the risk include deriving templates (numerical values extracted from an image and used for matching) from the original images as soon as possible, safely discarding the originals, and storing templates in a form that is technically infeasible or difficult to convert back into images.

On our reading, alternatives, consent and how long data is kept are the questions that follow from the PCPD's earlier guidance and the gaps in the reports, while the focus of the check given in the statement is data security. The good practices in the biometric guidance include offering an alternative where practicable, and its example is a smartcard in place of fingerprint-based attendance at work. In the guidance's workplace example, the Privacy Commissioner will not interfere where people have another option and choose to register voluntarily, and if the same standard is applied here, whether door cards are kept bears on whether Laguna City residents' registration counts as voluntary. The president of the Hong Kong Institute of Housing told Sing Tao Headline that biometric authentication would become the norm, but that residential facial recognition must have residents' consent and should come with other ways of verifying identity for residents to choose from.

Before registering their faces, Laguna City residents can ask the property management company whether they can keep using door cards, how consent will be given at registration and how long the data will be kept, since none of these appears in the reports or the company's responses. After registering, residents can make a data access request, asking the organisation for a copy of the personal data it holds about them, and anyone who suspects their privacy has been infringed can complain to the PCPD (telephone 2827 2827). Our reading is that, in answering a data access request, an organisation supplies only the data it acknowledges holding, so residents would still find it hard to confirm whether facial templates count as data they can request, or whether backups are deleted too. The company has said it will issue notices and hold sessions to explain how the system works before launch, and residents can raise these questions there.

A compliance check is not a finding of a breach. According to the PCPD's explanation of the ordinance, when the Privacy Commissioner receives a complaint or has reasonable grounds to believe the ordinance has been contravened, the decision whether to investigate depends on the facts and evidence. If an investigation finds a contravention, the Privacy Commissioner can issue an enforcement notice directing the organisation to take remedial steps, and failing to comply with the notice is an offence. This site's 9 November article covered [the plan to add facial recognition to the Hong Kong police's SmartView programme](https://anoni.net/news/en/2026/11/hk-mall-facial-recognition/), a police CCTV network, whereas the Laguna City system is being brought in by the management company of a private estate.

In mainland China, under a 2021 judicial interpretation by the Supreme People's Court, where a property management company makes facial recognition the only way for owners or occupants to enter the area it manages, courts will support those who do not agree and ask for another reasonable means of verification. The Measures for the Security Management of Facial Recognition Technology Applications (人脸识别技术应用安全管理办法), in force since 1 June 2025, apply to facial recognition in general and do not address residential estates specifically. Under these measures, facial recognition may not be the only means of verification where other non-facial-recognition methods can achieve the same purpose or meet the same business requirements, and people who do not consent must be offered another reasonable and convenient way. Facial data may also be kept no longer than the shortest time needed for the purpose of processing.

According to a September 2026 report in Yangtse Evening Post, a court in Chongqing's Yuzhong District ordered a property company that had installed facial recognition gates at an estate's only entrance to delete an owner's facial and ID card data and to provide another reasonable way in, and neither side appealed. In Hong Kong, the alternative appears in the PCPD's guidance as a recommended good practice, to be offered as far as practicable.

Next, it is worth watching whether the PCPD publishes the result of the check, and how the property management company explains door-card alternatives and the retention period for facial data in its sessions and notices before launch.
