Internet-connected smart TVs can use automatic content recognition (ACR) to record what viewers are watching. On 11 November The Verge's TV reviewer published 17 yes-or-no questions put to 11 manufacturers, along with their answers. Roku limited its answer to the US, and the others did not say which regions their answers cover. Samsung's privacy policies for Taiwan and Hong Kong do not state a default, and we, a community based in Taiwan, have not checked other brands' settings in either place.

According to another Verge report, from 4 November, ACR samples the audio or video playing on the TV and turns it into a digital fingerprint that is matched against a database to identify the programme. Manufacturers use the matches to recommend content, measure audiences and serve ads.

Of the 11 companies approached, Amazon, Apple, Google, LG, Roku, Samsung and TCL answered. According to The Verge, Hisense did not respond after five attempts, and Panasonic, Sony and Walmart's Vizio provided no answers to any of the 17 questions. All manufacturer answers below come from The Verge's report.

Apple answered that Apple TV 4K does not use ACR, and Google that the Google TV operating system itself does not use ACR to capture on-screen video, adding that TV makers may install separate ACR software. Samsung's answer was that its TVs do not capture or store "screenshots, screen recordings, or video recordings". Samsung's Taiwan privacy policy (effective 1 February 2024), however, says ACR may be used to capture viewing information, and the Hong Kong English page carries the same sentence: "Your Smart TV transfers video snippets or TV tuner information in order to determine the programs watched." LG answered that its ACR uses audio fingerprints generated by the TV's internal soundboard and does not capture images.

Samsung answered that viewing information services, which include ACR, are not enabled by default and require the user to opt in. LG and Amazon also answered that ACR is off by default, and Roku that its equivalent feature, Smart TV Experience, is off by default in the US. TCL attributed ACR to the operating system provider or third-party software, which users choose to turn on or not at initial setup and can turn off later.

The Verge's 4 November report describes a Northeastern University study (we did not obtain the paper itself) in which researchers monitored 12 TVs from 10 manufacturers to test whether opting out of ACR works. Most TVs stopped sending data to suspected ACR addresses after opt-out, while Samsung, Roku and Vizio TVs still showed related traffic. According to the paper as quoted by The Verge, almost all traffic to those addresses was encrypted. The researchers could not confirm whether the traffic remaining after opt-out was ACR data, or whether data was processed or retained on the server side.

## Perspective {#perspective}

According to The Verge's account, every TV with ACR traffic scanned over-the-air broadcasts received by antenna. On all but the Google and Amazon TVs, ACR also covered the manufacturer's own streaming services. HDMI inputs (where set-top boxes and game consoles plug in) were in scope too, while third-party apps such as Netflix and YouTube showed almost no ACR traffic. A separate 2024 paper for the Internet Measurement Conference (IMC) tested Samsung and LG, and found that ACR kept running when the TV was used only as an external display.

Samsung told The Verge that after opt-out the TV immediately stops collecting new viewing data and previously collected ACR data is deleted from its servers. In The Verge's account of the Northeastern study, traffic from Samsung's Tizen (Samsung's TV operating system) fell after opt-out but did not stop, and was still present when watching via antenna, first-party streaming and HDMI. When the 2024 IMC paper tested Samsung, traffic to ACR servers stopped after opt-out, and the two measurements used different models at different times.

By the same account, on the two TVs running Roku OS (one Roku model and one TCL model), ACR still applied to Roku's own The Roku Channel after opt-out. TCL discloses this limitation in its menus, while the Roku TV's menus do not. Roku's ACR Service Policy says that turning off Smart TV Experience does not affect data collected when content is accessed or streamed over the internet, and that data collected while it was on is retained by Roku and may still be shared with third parties.

By the same account, opting out on the Vizio TV made no difference to the related traffic. After the report was published, a Vizio spokesperson sent a statement denying that the encrypted traffic the researchers observed was ACR data, describing it as routine operational data such as diagnostics and performance.

The researchers also found a vulnerability in Vidaa OS (used on TVs from Hisense, Toshiba and other brands) that let them receive ACR results from other Vidaa TVs, by the same account. Each message carried a permanent TV identifier that cannot be reset, the input source and an audio fingerprint, and in three hours the researchers collected data from more than 1 million TVs. Vidaa confirmed the flaw and added a login requirement, but as of the report's publication on 4 November the messages were still unencrypted.

The Texas Attorney General reached agreements with Samsung in February and LG in May. The 26 February press release says Samsung must obtain Texas consumers' express consent before collecting or processing ACR viewing data. The 11 May release describes the LG settlement as protecting Texans, requiring informed consent, a pop-up disclosure on TV updates and "a clear and simple way to opt out". The releases do not say whether TVs elsewhere get the same update, the cases against Sony, Hisense and TCL were still pending as of 11 May, and we found nothing on them since.

Samsung's US support page says ACR runs only if the user opted in to viewing information services when setting up the TV, and that it can be turned off at any time in the Settings menu, though the page gives no menu path. Samsung's Taiwan privacy policy says viewing information services can be disabled at any time from the settings menu, and the Hong Kong English policy page says the same. Roku's policy page gives the path for US models as Settings > Privacy > Smart TV Experience, and personalised ads continue after it is turned off. We have not checked other brands' menu paths in Taiwan, so readers should go by the TV's own menus and each brand's official instructions.

We found no guidance or enforcement specific to smart TV ACR from regulators in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore or mainland China (one search, 14 November). Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act has no provision specific to targeted advertising. Mainland China's Personal Information Protection Law (Article 24) requires that information pushes and commercial marketing based on automated decision-making come with an option not targeted at personal characteristics or a convenient way to refuse, and we found no guidance applying it to TVs. Samsung's Singapore privacy policy, unlike its Taiwan and Hong Kong pages, contains no mention of viewing information services or ACR.

According to The Verge's account, the Northeastern researchers recommend as the easiest step disconnecting the TV from both wired and wireless networks and using a streaming device that does not run ACR instead. For people who want to keep using built-in apps, the researchers suggest webOS, Fire TV, Google TV on Sony and Vidaa (the system with the vulnerability above), opting out of everything possible, and they also list endpoints that can be blocked with Pi-hole (an open-source tool that blocks specified domains on a home network). On 13 November we also covered [Android TV boxes with preinstalled backdoors](https://anoni.net/news/en/2026/11/android-tv-residential-proxy/) being rented out as residential proxies.

People who do not want to rely on manufacturers honouring the settings can disconnect the TV from the network, at the cost of every built-in streaming app (our inference). Amazon and Google told The Verge that while offline a TV may store data or diagnostic logs locally and send them once reconnected. Roku said some usage data may be held on the device until the connection returns or the device is turned off, and TCL said that unless the user has opted out, the TV stores diagnostic and performance data offline and processes it once reconnected.
