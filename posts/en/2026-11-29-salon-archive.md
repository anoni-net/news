The US online magazine Salon announced on 5 November that it is ceasing publication, 31 years after it launched in November 1995. A week later, the Internet Archive posted on its blog that more than 250,000 Salon articles can be found through the Wayback Machine, and launched an experimental interface for finding them, Salon Explorer. People who have read or cited Salon's reporting still have somewhere to find the original after the closure.

In its farewell notice, Salon wrote that the business realities of independent digital journalism have changed profoundly over the years and that its own publishing model is no longer sustainable. As of 29 November, it is unclear how long Salon's old articles will remain on `salon.com`. After a publication stops, its website may stay up, or it may disappear once the hosting or the domain is no longer renewed. Once the site is gone, whether its old stories can still be found depends on whether anyone archived them in time.

According to the announcement, the Internet Archive has been preserving Salon's pages in the Wayback Machine for nearly three decades. In Salon Explorer, the work of more than 20,000 writers can be filtered by writer, year and era, and the full text can be searched by keyword. Readers used to the Wayback Machine can also enter a `salon.com` address directly and browse captures of the page by date.

## Perspective {#perspective}

Web archiving services mostly rely on crawlers, programs that visit web pages automatically and download their content, to save copies of pages at regular intervals and stamp each copy with the time it was taken. If nobody thinks of saving a site until it has closed, there is no page left to save.

Readers can submit the stories they care about to the Wayback Machine's Save Page Now while those stories are still online. According to the Internet Archive's help page, you paste a URL into the form and press the button, and the page is saved and given a permanent URL. There are extensions for Chrome, Firefox and Safari, as well as apps for iOS and Android. A few sites that prohibit crawling or have unusual SSL (encrypted connection) settings cannot be saved this way, and the Internet Archive does not keep the submitter's IP address.

To keep archives in your own hands, we covered SingleFile and ArchiveBox in our piece on [Spain's block on archive.today](https://anoni.net/news/en/2026/09/spain-blocks-archive-today/). SingleFile is a browser extension that saves a whole page as a single HTML file, and ArchiveBox is a self-hosted web archiving tool. Another option is ArchiveWeb.page, an open-source tool from Webrecorder that comes as an extension for Chrome and Chromium-based browsers and as a desktop app, and saves pages as you browse them. ArchiveBox and ArchiveWeb.page can both save in WARC, the standard format for web archives, which stores each URL together with the time it was fetched and the server's response, so that archive tools can later replay the page as it was.

Pages submitted to the Wayback Machine stay accessible only as long as the Internet Archive's service keeps running. Files saved on your own computer need their own backups, and because the only copy is in your hands, others have no way to confirm it has not been altered since you saved it.

Whether a closed news site can still be read depends both on whether anyone archived it in time and on whether readers can reach the archive from where they are. As of 29 November, GreatFire, which measures blocking from mainland China, has recorded interference with `web.archive.org` there since 14 April 2012, and found the site blocked in its latest conclusive test, on 7 November. In Hong Kong, according to records kept by the volunteer group Archive Team, the online outlet Stand News shut down after a police raid on 29 December 2021, its main site was replaced with a closure notice and its social media profiles were deleted. Before that, the site's sitemaps had been sent for archiving once in June and once in December, just before the shutdown, though a sitemap only lists a site's pages and does not guarantee that every one of them was captured.

Pages that others need to verify and cite are a good fit for Save Page Now, while pages you want to keep yourself suit ArchiveWeb.page or ArchiveBox. With the Wayback Machine extension for Chrome, for example, you click its toolbar icon on a page and choose Save Page Now to submit it. Save Page Now saves only the page you submit, so a story's other pages and the pages it links to have to be submitted separately. Anyone can look up Wayback Machine captures, so pages that require a login or contain personal data are not suitable for submission.
