On 21 September, Privacy International (PI), a UK privacy advocacy group, published a guide to 12 categories of surveillance technology used by immigration and border authorities, from mobile phone extraction, drones and facial recognition to GPS tagging and algorithmic decision-making. It draws on a 2022 PI survey of immigration lawyers, advocates and organisers around the world, and the report says the list is not exhaustive. Migrants, asylum seekers and the people who help them are directly affected, while the fingerprints and face photos that ordinary travellers give at borders belong to the biometrics category.

For each category the report covers how the technology works, its risks, companies reported to be involved and examples by country. Phone extraction can recover photos, contacts, social media accounts, deleted content and metadata such as where a photo was taken, and survey responses describe its use to assess asylum claims, build risk profiles and prosecute. The report names Cellebrite and cites the company's website saying its UFED phone extraction device can get past encryption and locks. Citing earlier PI research, it puts GPS tag accuracy at about 10 metres, with errors of 100 metres or more when signals bounce off tall buildings, which it says could lead to unjust detention or deportation.

The report also finds most algorithmic tools in migration cases secret, so it is unclear how far officers are involved or whether people can meaningfully contest the outcome. Other categories include dialect recognition software used to infer a person's country of origin, bone and dental scans for age assessment, and IMSI catchers, devices that pose as mobile masts so nearby phones connect and can be located, with some able to intercept texts and calls. According to the report, bones and teeth do not mature at a constant rate, so scans have no sound scientific basis for age and risk treating a child as an adult. The PDF cover is dated February, and some of it has moved on since: the report describes plans to add faces to Eurodac, the EU database that compares asylum seekers' fingerprints, while eu-LISA, which runs it, announced that the new Eurodac went live on 12 June handling both fingerprints and facial images.

## Perspective {#perspective}

PI's report says data from a phone may be out of context or out of date, and a second-hand or shared phone may even hold someone else's data. It adds that the power imbalance between officials and the person concerned undermines meaningful consent. Cellebrite's ethics page says the company vets potential customers' human rights records, and customers that breach its licence terms lose product support and licence renewals. Germany's Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF) says on its identity management page that it reads phone metadata only when identity cannot be established by milder means such as identity documents, using the countries called and location data from certain apps to infer origin.

The same BAMF page says that when applicants cannot produce identity documents, an automated analysis of a speech sample can narrow down the region they come from. It also says these technical aids do not replace the applicant's own account or BAMF experts' assessment of all the evidence. The critics PI cites argue that dialects do not follow borders and that closely related languages are easily confused. The report adds that giving interviewers a result before the asylum interview could colour their judgement.

In studies cited by PI, aerial surveillance does not simply deter crossings but pushes people onto more dangerous routes. The report also says drones can facilitate pushbacks, forcing people back across a border and frustrating their right to seek asylum. Frontex, the EU border agency, says on its search and rescue page that its planes and drones patrol selected areas beyond the EU's external borders as an early warning mechanism, and that it alerts the responsible national rescue authority when it spots a boat in distress. According to Airbus, the Heron drones that its subsidiary and Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI) operate for Frontex send data straight to Frontex's command centre in Warsaw and to host countries, to coordinate rescues in real time and act against illegal activities.

The UK Home Office's immigration bail guidance, version 23.0 published on 18 September, says bail conditions should let the Home Office keep in contact with a person and reduce the risk of absconding, and that electronic monitoring must not be imposed on anyone under 18. Under the guidance, location trail data is held by the monitoring contractor and accessed by the Home Office only in listed cases such as a breach of bail conditions, loss of contact or a specific request from a law enforcement agency, with regular reviews of whether monitoring is still necessary. PI's report says people who are tagged reportedly suffer stress, sleeplessness and anxiety, and devices can cause bruises and blisters.

In March 2024 the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), the UK data protection regulator, found that a Home Office pilot tagging up to 600 people who had arrived in the UK breached data protection law. The ICO said the Home Office had not assessed the intrusion of continuous location tracking or told people clearly what was collected and how it would be used. It also said it was for the Home Office to decide what measures are necessary, and that the problem was the department's failure to justify necessity and to show it had considered less intrusive methods.

PI's report says Canada uses automated decision-making at various stages of visa processing, on questions including whether an application is complete, whether the applicant is a risk and whether the stated basis is truthful. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) said in January 2022 that for temporary resident visa applications from outside Canada the system sorts files, letting routine ones be assessed 87% faster. According to IRCC, the system can find an applicant eligible but never refuses or recommends refusing an application. It also says an algorithmic impact assessment under the federal Directive on Automated Decision-Making was completed, discriminatory impacts were reviewed, and officers can overturn an eligibility finding.

Several places in East Asia collect biometrics from foreign visitors in general, a wider group than the asylum seekers and irregular arrivals covered by Eurodac. In Taiwan, Article 91 of the Immigration Act lets the National Immigration Agency collect biometric data from foreign nationals and several other groups when they cross the border or apply for residence, with under-14s exempt and entry refusable if a person declines. Japan's Immigration Services Agency requires foreign nationals applying for landing to provide fingerprints and a facial photograph unless exempted by law, and refusal leads to a hearing before a special inquiry officer. In mainland China, the Ministry of Public Security began taking fingerprints from foreign nationals aged 14 to 70 at entry in 2017, according to an August 2017 Xinhua report.

The stated purposes differ as well. eu-LISA describes the new Eurodac as a tool for the reliable identification of asylum seekers and irregular migrants in asylum and migration management. South Korea, which has required fingerprints and facial photos from foreign nationals aged 17 and over since 2012, gave its aims in a 2011 notice as keeping out foreign criminals and terrorists and identifying foreigners involved in accidents or crimes. Japan's agency describes landing examination as a way to stop the landing of undesirable foreign nationals, including those with doubtful purposes of entry. On the forensics side, Cellebrite's ethics page says it has chosen not to do business in mainland China, Hong Kong and Macau, partly over human rights and data security concerns.

Most readers have nothing to change. People who help migrants or asylum seekers through legal procedures can start with the PDF, which runs to 47 pages in English only, needs no registration and may be shared and translated for non-commercial use. On privacy, compare how long data is kept, who can access it, and whether people are told and can challenge the outcome. On the stated aims, compare whether these tools actually make identity checks faster and more accurate, and whether monitoring helps rescues or reduces absconding.
