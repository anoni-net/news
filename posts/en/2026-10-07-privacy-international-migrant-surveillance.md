On 21 September, Privacy International (PI), a UK privacy advocacy group, published a guide to 12 categories of surveillance technology used by immigration and border authorities, from mobile phone extraction, drones and facial recognition to GPS tagging and algorithmic decision-making. It draws on a 2022 PI survey of immigration lawyers, advocates and organisers around the world, and the report says the list is not exhaustive. Migrants, asylum seekers and the people who help them are directly affected, while the fingerprints and face photos that ordinary travellers give at borders belong to the biometrics category.

For each category the report covers how the technology works, its risks, companies reported to be involved and examples by country. Phone extraction can recover photos, contacts, social media accounts, deleted content and metadata such as where a photo was taken, and survey responses describe its use to assess asylum claims, build risk profiles and prosecute. The report names Cellebrite and cites the company's website saying its UFED phone extraction device can get past encryption and other locks.

GPS tagging uses ankle tags, smartwatches or other devices, and some forms let authorities see a wearer's live location at all times. Citing earlier PI research, the report says GPS tag accuracy appears limited to around 10 metres, with errors of 100 metres or more when signals bounce off tall buildings. Such errors could lead to misreadings of a person's movements and so risk unjust detention or deportation.

The report also finds most algorithmic tools in migration cases secret, so it is unclear how far officers are involved or whether people can meaningfully contest the outcome.

Other categories include dialect recognition software used to infer a person's country of origin, which critics cited in the report say is unreliable because dialects do not always follow borders, and bone and dental scans for age assessment. According to the report, bones and teeth do not mature at a constant rate, so scans have no sound scientific basis for age and risk treating a child as an adult.

The PDF cover is dated February, and some of it has moved on since. The report describes plans to add faces to Eurodac, the EU database that compares asylum seekers' fingerprints. eu-LISA, which runs it, announced that the new Eurodac went live on 12 June handling both fingerprints and facial images.

## Perspective {#perspective}

PI's report says data from a phone may be out of context or out of date, and a second-hand or shared phone may even hold someone else's data. It adds that the power imbalance between officials and the person concerned could undermine meaningful consent. Germany's Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF) says on its identity management page that it reads phone metadata, such as the countries called and location data from certain apps, only when identity cannot be established by less intrusive means such as identity documents. The same page says these technical aids do not replace the applicant's own account or BAMF experts' assessment of all the evidence.

In studies cited by PI, aerial surveillance does not simply deter crossings but also pushes people into more dangerous areas and routes, raising the risk of injury or death. The report also says drones can facilitate pushbacks, forcing people back across a border and frustrating their right to seek asylum. Frontex, the EU border agency, says on its search and rescue page that its planes and drones patrol selected areas beyond the EU's external borders as an early warning mechanism, and that it alerts the responsible national rescue authority when it spots a boat in distress.

The UK Home Office's immigration bail guidance, updated in September, says bail conditions should let the Home Office keep in contact with a person and reduce the risk of absconding, and that electronic monitoring must not be imposed on anyone under 18. Under the guidance, location trail data is held by the monitoring contractor and accessed by the Home Office only in listed cases such as a breach of bail conditions, loss of contact or a specific request from a law enforcement agency, with regular reviews of whether monitoring is still necessary. PI's report says people who are tagged reportedly suffer stress, sleeplessness and anxiety, and devices can cause bruises and blisters.

In March 2024 the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), the UK data protection regulator, found that a Home Office pilot tagging up to 600 people who had arrived in the UK breached data protection law. In the ICO's finding, the Home Office had not sufficiently assessed the privacy intrusion and could not justify the pilot as necessary. The ICO also said it was for the Home Office to decide what measures are necessary.

PI's report says Canada uses automated decision-making at various stages of visa processing, on questions including whether an application is complete, whether the applicant is a risk and whether the stated basis is truthful. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) said in January 2022 that for temporary resident visa applications from outside Canada the system sorts files, letting routine ones be assessed 87% faster. According to IRCC, the system can find an applicant eligible but never refuses or recommends refusing an application, and officers can overturn an eligibility finding.

The EU and several places in East Asia also collect biometrics from ordinary foreign visitors, outside any asylum procedure. According to the European Commission, the EU's Entry/Exit System (EES), which began progressive operations on 12 October 2025 and became fully operational on 10 April 2026, records fingerprints and facial images of non-EU nationals on short stays in 29 European countries. In Taiwan, under Article 91 of the Immigration Act, the National Immigration Agency may collect biometric data from foreign nationals and several other groups when they cross the border or apply for residence, with under-14s exempt and entry refusable if a person declines. Japan requires foreign nationals applying for landing to provide fingerprints and a facial photograph unless exempted by law, and mainland China began fingerprinting foreign nationals aged 14 to 70 at entry in 2017, according to an August 2017 Xinhua report.

The stated purposes point in different directions. The Commission says the EES replaces passport stamps so that overstayers are detected automatically, and that it helps prevent irregular migration and catch people using forged documents, while eu-LISA describes Eurodac as a tool for identifying asylum seekers and irregular migrants. Japan's Immigration Services Agency describes landing examination as a way to stop the landing of undesirable foreign nationals, including those whose purpose of entry is doubtful. Taiwan's Article 91 sets out who is covered and leaves the details of collection, management and use to implementing rules.

Most readers have nothing to change. People who help migrants or asylum seekers through legal procedures can start with the PDF, which runs to 47 pages in English only, needs no registration and may be shared and translated for non-commercial use. On privacy, compare how long data is kept, who can access it, and whether people are told and can challenge the outcome. On the stated aims, compare whether these tools actually make identity checks faster and more accurate, and whether monitoring helps rescues or reduces absconding.
