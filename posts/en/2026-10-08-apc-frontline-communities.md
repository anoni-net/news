APC (the Association for Progressive Communications, an international network of civil society organisations) published a preview on 22 September of research from Brazil, Kenya, Mexico and the Philippines. It covers environmental defenders resisting mining, energy and other extractive projects, many of them from Indigenous and traditional communities, and the online attacks they face. The full reports launch at an online event on Zoom at 13:00 UTC on 8 October (21:00 Taipei time), with interpretation in English, Spanish and Portuguese. The research is limited to those four countries; what readers elsewhere can take from it is how communities respond to harassment and disinformation together.

The studies use the term technology-facilitated violence for harm committed, assisted or intensified through digital platforms: hate speech, cyberbullying, surveillance, disinformation, impersonation, doxing and direct cyberattacks. The summary reports, released in June, note that attacks reach defenders' families, friends and colleagues too, creating a chilling effect. APC's article argues that perpetrators benefit from the design and algorithms of major platforms, and that Big Tech's dominance of internet services has deepened the impact of disinformation where local media have been decimated.

All four studies found a digital divide, with limited internet access, devices and skills leaving communities more exposed to disinformation, fraud, surveillance and attacks. The research began in 2024 with more than 20 Indigenous communities across the four countries, using feminist participatory methods with community researchers at every stage. Responses documented include communities running their own radio station, social media accounts and intranet, and setting their own digital protection practices.

## Perspective {#perspective}

In one of the country surveys, 60% of defenders who experienced online attacks reported them to technology companies and 29% blocked the perpetrators. A significant number took no action, citing attackers hidden behind anonymous or fake accounts, the absence of clear reporting channels and fear of retaliation. Reporting and blocking deal with one account at a time, and an attacker can simply open another.

The same report recommends a security risk assessment that identifies threats and vulnerabilities, followed by a tailored security plan. It also calls for peer support networks to help defenders manage the psychological toll of online violence.

Another report found that phones are often shared among family members, neighbours and co-workers, so the research team treats digital security as a household and community matter. The only protective practices participants named were setting social media profiles to private and avoiding topics that could provoke attacks, which the report notes is itself a form of silencing. When attacks spread to relatives and colleagues, turning on two-factor authentication for one's own account is not enough, and the people who share a phone or are named together need to plan together.

Running a community radio station or social media accounts lets a community answer disinformation in its own voice. The same research records a case where a local radio station's content was taken down by a platform, a reminder that anything hosted on someone else's platform is subject to that platform's rules. A self-hosted intranet avoids those rules, but the community then carries the cost of equipment, power and maintenance, the kind of true cost APC's article says needs better evaluation.

In the Philippines, the one Asian country in the study, the summary report describes red-tagging, labelling people as communist insurgents or terrorists without substantial evidence, as a central mechanism of repression that social media amplifies. Human Rights Watch reported that on 8 May 2024 the Philippine Supreme Court declared red-tagging a threat to life, liberty and security. That allows people who are targeted to seek a writ of amparo, a court remedy that can include protection orders.

In Taiwan, Cofacts runs a message-reporting chatbot backed by a crowd-sourced fact-checking community. Users add `@cofacts` as a friend on LINE and forward suspicious messages to its chatbot, which returns fact-checks written by volunteer editors. Its code is open source under the MIT licence and was still being updated on 30 September, and the site states that editors' fact-checks are not necessarily correct or complete. GreatFire, which tests website blocking in mainland China, shows both `cofacts.tw` and `line.me` blocked there as of 1 October.

A first step for planning with the people around you is EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense guide Your Security Plan. It walks through six questions: what you want to protect, from whom, how bad the consequences of failure would be, how likely they are, how much trouble you are willing to take, and who your allies are. The guide is free and needs no account. The English version was last reviewed on 27 October 2023, and the Simplified Chinese translation follows that revision, while the Thai and Vietnamese translations date from January 2019 and the Burmese one from January 2021. There is no Traditional Chinese version.
