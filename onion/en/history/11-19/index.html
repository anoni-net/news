Penalties for online posts in Taiwan, mainland China and Hong Kong, and the arrests in India over a Facebook post and a like

19 November 2012

A post you write on social media or a forum can be punished under different laws in Taiwan, mainland China and Hong Kong: Taiwan's Social Order Maintenance Act allows up to three days' detention or a fine of up to NT$30,000 for spreading rumours likely to disturb public peace (Article 63, paragraph 1, subparagraph 5), and a 2013 judicial interpretation by mainland China's Supreme People's Court and Supreme People's Procuratorate sets the threshold for "serious circumstances" under the defamation offence in Article 246 of the Criminal Law at 5,000 views or 500 forwards of an online post. In Hong Kong, police arrested six people in a national security case in May 2024 on suspicion of "offences in connection with seditious intention" under Section 24 of the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance, over posts on a Facebook page that the Hong Kong government said advocated hatred against the central government, the Hong Kong SAR Government and the Judiciary (we checked the Taiwanese and Chinese texts and the Hong Kong government's report on 14 November 2026). In November 2012, a woman in Palghar, near Mumbai, questioned on Facebook why the whole city had shut down for a politician's funeral, and police arrested her and a second woman who had liked the post under the Indian Penal Code and Section 66A of India's Information Technology Act, 2000 (the Indian news agency PTI dated the arrests to Sunday the 18th, BBC News to Monday the 19th); both were released on bail on the 19th, when PTI reported that the arrests had sparked outrage and that the chairman of the Press Council of India had demanded immediate action against the police officers involved. Section 66A made it an offence to send, through a computer or communication device, messages that were "grossly offensive" or that the sender knew to be false and sent to cause "annoyance" or "inconvenience", and on 24 March 2015 India's Supreme Court struck down the whole section because terms such as "annoyance" and "inconvenience" were undefined and could restrict a very large amount of protected speech (the wording and the court's reasoning follow the summary in Columbia University's Global Freedom of Expression case database; the judgment itself does not mention these arrests). According to the Indian digital rights group Internet Freedom Foundation and the same database, the Supreme Court ordered on 12 October 2022 that cases still pending under the section after the ruling be closed and that no new cases be registered under it.
