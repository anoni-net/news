CSWP 50, NIST's cybersecurity guide for one-person businesses

On 2 November NIST published CSWP 50, a guide for one-person businesses with no employees that sets out actions such as multi-factor authentication, backups, updates and recognising phishing, written for readers with no security background. The guide is free to download, and as of 15 November we found only an English version. It is written for a US audience, though freelancers and self-employed people elsewhere can draw on its practices.

On 2 November the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published CSWP 50, Small Business Cybersecurity: Non-Employer Firms, a guide for one-person businesses with no employees, where the owner is the only paid worker. According to the press release, the guide is written in lay language and assumes no prior cybersecurity knowledge. It is final and free to download, and as of 15 November we found only an English version.

According to the guide, it was developed for a US audience, though many small businesses engage in international commerce or collaborations and the content can be adapted to support that work. Freelancers and self-employed people outside the US can draw on its practices. We infer that the US laws and support programmes it mentions do not apply elsewhere, and readers will need to substitute local equivalents.

According to the press release, more than 28 million small businesses in the US are one-person businesses. In the guide's abstract, the audience is "non-employer" firms as defined by the US Small Business Administration (SBA), meaning businesses with no employees other than the owner, and the guide may also be useful to businesses with very few employees or minimal IT infrastructure. The section on audience covers sole proprietors, freelancers, single-member limited liability companies (LLCs), independent contractors and gig economy workers, and counts the consultants that many small businesses rely on as another key audience.

According to the press release, the earlier guide, NISTIR 7621, was aimed at small businesses of varying sizes, while the revised CSWP 50 concentrates on protecting digital systems from malicious attack. The guide is based on version 2.0 of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF 2.0, the structure NIST uses to organise an organisation's cybersecurity work) and is much more detailed than the CSF quick-start guide for small businesses. In the middle section, as the press release describes it, are actions a one-person business can take, each with the reason for it, how to get started and options once the business grows. The last part has a worksheet for inventorying assets, a table for tracking completed actions, and three hypothetical cases (a lawyer, an artisan and a business consultant), which are in a separate appendix file.

The guide's introduction has a list of nine actions that can significantly reduce risk. The first is to turn on phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication (MFA, which asks for a second form of verification besides the password at login) on accounts that offer it, especially email, banking, password managers and critical cloud services. Following NIST's guidance, passwords should be at least 15 characters long, a password manager is worth considering, and where possible passkeys can be used instead (a passkey is a login credential unlocked with a fingerprint, face or PIN on the device, with no username and password to type).

The other actions are learning to recognise phishing and ransomware, using a standard user account for routine work, inventorying important assets and the services the business depends on, setting up and testing backups, and keeping all devices and apps updated. The last two are listing the internal and external contacts to notify when an incident happens and keeping a printed copy, and listing the laws, regulations and contractual requirements that relate to cybersecurity. The ransomware section advises securing and isolating backups of important data, and the backup item in Chapter 3 also suggests considering at least one copy on a medium that is not connected to the device, such as an external hard drive or the cloud.

The guide's advice is to avoid mixing personal and business assets where possible, keeping personal devices separate from business devices. Where a device has to be shared with family members, each person should have their own account, and family members' accounts should not have access to sensitive business data. Another point in the guide is that home, cafés and co-working spaces each carry different physical threats, and that devices should have location tracking and remote wipe turned on.

For requests to change bank account details, amend an invoice, make an urgent payment or buy gift cards, the guide advises confirming through known or public contact details rather than replying to the message itself. The phishing section also mentions that AI products and services can now be used to craft more convincing phishing messages, and the guide calls them "Super Intelligence" (SI).

A footnote in the guide explains that SI follows a US executive order of 29 September, which directed executive agencies to change their usage from "artificial intelligence" to "super intelligence". A separate section on AI tools recommends taking stock of the AI tools in use and the AI features built into other software. Before entering sensitive information, users should check the settings for data retention, use for training and access permissions, and AI-generated content should be reviewed before it is used.

Perspective

In the guide's explanation of MFA, turning it on for every account that offers it is one of the most important steps for protecting a business. The same explanation mentions that some forms of MFA are susceptible to phishing, and gives passkeys as the phishing-resistant example. In common phishing techniques, a one-time code from a text message or an authenticator app can be captured by a fake site and passed on. A passkey is bound to the site where it was registered, so a fake site has difficulty obtaining a usable credential.

The press release quotes one of the authors as saying that many of the actions can be carried out on one's own with limited technical knowledge or minimal financial investment. The guide mentions only in passing that a password manager is a relatively inexpensive approach, and it does not estimate the cost of each action. Instead of doing the work themselves, owners can also hand it to a consultant or an outside provider, and the guide cautions that outsourcing does not transfer the responsibility for protecting systems and data.

Readers who want a Chinese-language counterpart can turn to the guidelines on basic cybersecurity protection for small and medium-sized enterprises, or SMEs (中小企業基本資安防護指引) from Taiwan's Administration for Cyber Security, part of the Ministry of Digital Affairs, which carries a cover date of March 2026. The 11-page PDF covers three areas (account management, device and data management, and security awareness) and comes with a self-assessment checklist. It is written for SMEs, with nothing separate for one-person businesses. Comparing the two documents, we found that the checklist's items on passwords of at least 15 characters, changing default passwords on connected devices, backups, automatic updates and an asset inventory all overlap with NIST's recommendations.

We, a community based in Taiwan, also looked at guidance from public bodies in Japan, Singapore and Hong Kong (checked on 15 November), and like Taiwan's, each is aimed at SMEs in general rather than at one-person businesses. Japan's Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA) published version 4.0 of its guidelines on information security measures for SMEs (中小企業の情報セキュリティ対策ガイドライン) on 27 March 2026, a 70-page main volume in Japanese, while the English edition linked from that page is version 3.1. The Cyber Security Agency of Singapore (CSA) offers toolkits for enterprise leaders and SME owners, aimed at businesses that may not have dedicated IT staff and listed on its page as English PDFs. In Hong Kong, the Hong Kong Computer Emergency Response Team Coordination Centre (HKCERT) released Seven Habits of Cyber Security for SMEs, which includes a self-assessment checklist, in September 2018, and an Incident Response Guideline for SMEs, also written for non-technical readers, in July 2022.

People with no employees who look after all their own accounts and devices can start by checking their situation against the nine actions in the introduction, one at a time. The guide is a free 33-page English PDF on the NIST website, with the inventory worksheets and the three cases in a separate .xlsx file. The press release also quotes one of the authors as saying that the guide will not address all the needs of every one-person business. According to Chapter 3, the actions in its tables are not intended as a checklist or compliance criteria, and privacy is mostly outside the guide's scope apart from a few basic steps.
